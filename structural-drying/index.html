Structural drying is the most important phase of water damage restoration, and the one untrained responders most often get wrong. Once standing water has been extracted, a great deal of moisture is still held inside the structure: in insulation, concrete, subfloors, wood framing, drywall and other components. This trapped moisture must be scientifically removed through controlled evaporation and dehumidification to prevent the mold growth, wood rot, material degradation and odor problems that develop when structures remain wet.

Psychrometry is the science of how air temperature, humidity and moisture interact, and the IICRC-certified technicians who dry structures for Vallejo Restoration know it well. Using this knowledge, they calculate the specific equipment placement, temperature settings and airflow patterns needed to create the right evaporation conditions for each drying situation. Different materials (drywall, hardwood, concrete, plaster) dry at different rates and need different approaches to reach acceptable moisture levels without causing additional damage.

Every day of the drying process, we check moisture using thermal imaging cameras, thermo-hygrometers and calibrated moisture meters. Readings are recorded from start to finish in a detailed drying log, which shows moisture dropping step by step in every affected material. Those readings guide daily equipment adjustments that keep the drying as efficient as it can be. A surface that feels dry to your hand proves nothing, so the job is finished only once all affected materials test within the acceptable standards set by IICRC S500.

Why Hire a Professional for Structural Drying?

Drying a structure properly takes science, and guesswork has no place in it. Secondary mold damage most often traces back to someone who put down fans and hoped things would dry. Professional structural drying requires understanding how different materials absorb and release moisture, calculating the grain depression (moisture removal capacity) each situation needs, and monitoring daily to confirm drying is progressing properly. Lacking that understanding, people either stop too soon and leave moisture for mold, or dry too hard and harm wood and other materials.

The equipment used for professional structural drying is fundamentally different from consumer products. Commercial LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers remove 10 to 20 times more moisture from the air than residential dehumidifiers, and high-velocity air movers create the precise airflow patterns needed to accelerate evaporation from structural surfaces. Machines like these cost a great deal and take training to use well, which explains why professional results are so much better than a do-it-yourself effort.

How to Tell You Need Structural Drying

Catching these issues early helps you avoid costly problems and protect your property. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Materials that feel damp or cool to the touch even after visible water is gone
  • Swollen trim and baseboards separating at the seams
  • Signs of deterioration where an old leak was left to dry on its own
  • Stained, swollen or soft spots in the drywall
  • After water damage, a meter shows elevated moisture in the floor, wall or ceiling
  • Trapped moisture often announces itself with a persistent musty odor after cleanup
  • Rooms that stay humid even with windows open and fans running

Structural Drying Prevention Tips

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly water damage issues down the road. Here are our expert recommendations:

  • The dehumidifiers and air movers should run around the clock until our technician takes them out
  • Moisture can sit deep in a material whose surface feels dry, so rely on the meter readings
  • Dehumidifiers lose efficiency when outside air gets in, so keep drying areas closed up
  • New odors or wet areas during the drying period should be reported to your technician right away
  • Deal with water intrusion right away, since an early start on drying gives a better result
  • Plan for daily access so readings can be taken and equipment adjusted
